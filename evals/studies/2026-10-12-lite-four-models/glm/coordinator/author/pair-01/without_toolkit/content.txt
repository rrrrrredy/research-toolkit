# Reminder Pilot: Assessment Note

**Descriptive results (S1).** Among the 100 borrowers who chose reminders, on-time returns rose from 75% to 80% (a 5-percentage-point improvement). Among the 100 who declined, returns rose from 55% to 60% (also 5 points). The gap between groups was 20 points in both the prior month and the pilot month, so the *change over time* was identical in both groups. Reminder-group borrowers were already returning items on time at a much higher rate before the pilot began.

**Design assessment (S2).** Enrollment was voluntary, not randomized, so the two groups likely differ systematically — for example, in motivation or borrowing habits. A fine-amnesty campaign affected both groups during the pilot and may explain the common 5-point rise. Reasons for opting in and prior trends were not recorded. Because of these confounds and the absence of a randomized comparison, the pilot does **not** demonstrate a causal effect of reminders on on-time returns. The 20-point group gap is descriptive, not causal evidence.

**Proposed next test (S2).** Randomize an offer of reminders next month, recording assignment, uptake, and return status. Because staff did randomize assignment, an intention-to-treat comparison of on-time return rates between offered and not-offered borrowers would support a causal claim.

**Decision criterion (S2).** Before results are examined, management must define the smallest worthwhile benefit — I recommend 5 percentage points. If the randomized offer improves on-time returns by at least that margin, expand the program; otherwise, stop.

Sources: S1, S2.
