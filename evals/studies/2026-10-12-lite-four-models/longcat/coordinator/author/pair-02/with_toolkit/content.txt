# Vale Library Reminder Pilot: Assessment and Next Steps

## What the numbers show

The descriptive results are straightforward. Among the 100 borrowers who opted into reminders, on-time returns rose from 75 to 80 (a 5-percentage-point gain). Among the 100 who declined, returns rose from 55 to 60 (also 5 points) (S1). Both groups improved by the same amount, so the observed change is entirely shared between them.

## Why this does not demonstrate a causal effect

The pilot's design prevents a causal reading. Enrollment was voluntary, not randomized, so the two groups differed at baseline—75 on-time returns versus 55 in the prior month—suggesting the reminder group already contained more reliable returners (S2). That baseline gap means the 5-point gain could reflect pre-existing differences rather than the reminders themselves. In addition, a fine-amnesty campaign reached both groups during the pilot, offering a plausible alternative explanation for the shared improvement (S2). Without earlier trend data or a record of why borrowers opted in, selection bias cannot be ruled out (S2).

## Proposed next test

Staff can randomize an offer of reminders next month and record assignment, uptake, and return status (S2). Randomization would balance pre-existing reliability between groups, isolating the reminder's contribution from the amnesty's effect if the campaign is controlled or measured.

## Decision criterion

Before examining results, management should set the smallest worthwhile benefit—for example, a 10-percentage-point increase in on-time returns attributable to the reminder (S2). If the randomized trial meets or exceeds that threshold and the result is statistically distinguishable from zero, the library should adopt reminders; if not, the pilot evidence is insufficient to justify expansion.
