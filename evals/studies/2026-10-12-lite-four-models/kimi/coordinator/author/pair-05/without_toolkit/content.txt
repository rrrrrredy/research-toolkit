## Note to the Vale Library Director: Reminder Pilot Assessment

**Descriptive results.** On-time returns rose from 75% to 80% among borrowers who opted into reminders, and from 55% to 60% among those who declined (S1). Both groups improved by 5 percentage points.

**Causal assessment.** The pilot design cannot support causal claims. Enrollment was voluntary, not randomized, so unmeasured differences between groups likely confound any comparison (S2). The parallel 5-point improvement in both groups further suggests that a contemporaneous fine-amnesty campaign, which reached both groups, may explain the gains rather than reminders themselves (S1, S2). Without data on earlier trends or borrowers' reasons for opting in, selection bias remains unaddressed (S2).

**Recommended next test.** Staff should randomize the *offer* of reminders next month, then record assignment, actual uptake, and return status (S2). This permits intent-to-treat analysis and, with uptake data, exploratory complier-average causal effect estimation.

**Decision criterion.** Management must first specify the smallest worthwhile benefit—e.g., a 5 or 10 percentage-point improvement in on-time returns—before examining results (S2). If the lower bound of the 95% confidence interval for the intent-to-treat effect exceeds this threshold, implement reminders library-wide; otherwise, reconsider the intervention or test modifications.
