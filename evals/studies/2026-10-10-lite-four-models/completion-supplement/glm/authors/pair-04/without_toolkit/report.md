# Research Memo: U.S. Utility-Scale Battery Storage Market — Preliminary Read for Entry Decision

**To:** Strategy Lead
**Date:** August 10, 2026
**Subject:** What the EIA's August 7, 2026 storage-capacity data does — and does not — tell us, and the recommended next step
**Source basis:** S1 only (EIA, "Battery storage capacity averaged 70% growth over the last three years," August 7, 2026)

## 1. What the data says

The single supplied source establishes a clear and substantial expansion trend in U.S. utility-scale battery storage:

- Capacity grew at an annual average rate of 70% over the last three years, reaching 43.6 GW of operational capacity by the end of 2025 (S1).
- Operators added another 8.3 GW in the first half of 2026, bringing nameplate capacity to nearly 52 GW (S1).
- Operators report plans to bring an additional 54 GW online over the next two and a half years: 14 GW in the second half of 2026, 26 GW in 2027, and 14 GW in 2028 (S1).

The source also identifies the dominant siting pattern: large hybrid solar-plus-storage facilities. The Bellefield Solar and Energy Storage Farm (500 MW PV / 500 MW storage, operational December 2025, with plans to double both by November 2026), the Manatee Solar Energy Center (75 MW solar / 409 MW storage), and the Gemini Solar Hybrid (690 MW PV / 380 MW storage) are the three named examples (S1).

## 2. What this implies for demand

Taken at face value, the figures imply a large, near-term, and continued build-out. If reported plans materialize, the fleet would roughly double from ~52 GW to over 100 GW by end-2028 — a pace comparable to the 2026 first-half run rate sustained across three years. Every GW of nameplate storage capacity added requires power-conversion, power-management, and integration hardware — the product territory of a power-electronics supplier. The hybrid-solar pattern (S1) additionally suggests demand is concentrated in projects where storage is co-engineered with PV plants, which may favor suppliers able to serve hybrid interconnection and co-located designs. Even partial completion of the announced pipeline would represent a substantial equipment volume opportunity relative to a mid-sized supplier's scale.

## 3. What the data cannot establish

This is the critical limitation, and it bounds the decision we can responsibly make today.

**First, the figures are developer/operator plans, not commitments.** The source itself hedges: "If those plans materialize" (S1, on Bellefield's expansion). Announced pipelines in this industry routinely slip or cancel; the 54 GW figure is an upper-bound intention set, not a forecast of realized additions.

**Second, the data measures capacity (GW), not supplier revenue.** The source reports nameplate power capacity only. It contains no figures on storage duration (MWh), system prices, hardware content per GW, project costs, or margins. We therefore cannot convert 54 GW into an addressable-revenue estimate for our product lines. Any such number would be invented.

**Third, the data says nothing about project economics or returns.** The source notes only the general arbitrage logic — operators store power when wholesale prices are low and discharge when high, "allowing power producers to capture larger profits" (S1). It provides no actual price-spread data, revenue figures, IRRs, or margin evidence for any project. We cannot assess whether these projects will earn returns that sustain the build-out, or whether today's plans survive contact with financing conditions.

**Fourth, no competitive or supply-chain information.** The source is silent on who currently supplies the power electronics for these projects, incumbent share, or pricing dynamics. The opportunity size tells us nothing about whether a mid-sized entrant can win any of it.

## 4. Recommendation: authorize a limited market-discovery effort, not a commercial commitment

**Strongest opportunity signal:** the sheer momentum — 70% average annual growth over three years, ~52 GW already operational, and a reported 54 GW pipeline through 2028 (S1). This is a market in a sustained expansion phase with visible, dated near-term additions (14 GW in 2026 H2 alone).

**Strongest reason for caution:** the pipeline is entirely self-reported operator plans with no economics attached (S1). We cannot verify that these projects are financeable at prices that would support our entry, and we have zero evidence on our ability to win volume against incumbents.

The asymmetry is decisive: the upside is credible but unquantified; the risk is that neither revenue nor returns can be established from this source. The proportionate response is a **limited market-discovery effort** — mapping the named hybrid projects and reported 2026–2028 pipeline against our product qualifications, initiating conversations with developers and EPCs, and gathering pricing and specification evidence — **not** a larger commercial commitment such as dedicated capacity, an acquisition, or a dedicated U.S. business unit. A commitment of that scale cannot currently be justified by evidence in hand.

## 5. What evidence would change the recommendation

The recommendation should shift toward a larger commercial commitment if discovery produces:

1. **Realized-addition tracking**: confirmation that the 14 GW slated for 2026 H2 is actually reaching commercial operation on schedule, indicating the reported pipeline is converting.
2. **Priced, duration-adjusted demand data** (MWh and equipment-content evidence from real tenders or contracts), enabling a defensible addressable-revenue estimate.
3. **Evidence of winnable positions**: specification or qualification access on projects comparable to Bellefield, Manatee, or Gemini (S1), or documented incumbent gaps.

Conversely, the recommendation would shift toward deprioritization if the pipeline shows significant slippage or if discovery reveals locked-in incumbent supply relationships.
