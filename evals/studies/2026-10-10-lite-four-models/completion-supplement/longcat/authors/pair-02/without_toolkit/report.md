# Research Memo: U.S. Utility-Scale Battery-Storage Market

**To:** Strategy Lead
**From:** Market Analysis (desk research)
**Date:** August 10, 2026
**Re:** Demand signal from official U.S. capacity data, and recommended next step
**Sources:** S1 — U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA), "Battery storage capacity averaged 70% growth over the last three years," August 7, 2026.

**Scope.** This is a bounded, source-based desk memo prepared for a hypothetical mid-sized power-electronics supplier evaluating the U.S. utility-scale battery-storage market. It draws exclusively on the supplied EIA article (S1); it is not live research and not investment advice. Source facts are official material; the company and decision scenario are hypothetical. Consistent with the source, no supplier prices, storage durations, project margins, or probabilities are assumed in this memo.

**What the figures imply for demand (S1).** Operational battery storage capacity reached 43.6 GW by the end of 2025; operators added 8.3 GW in the first six months of 2026, bringing nameplate capacity to nearly 52 GW. This follows an annual average growth rate of 70% over the last three years. Operators anticipate a further 54 GW online over the next two and a half years — 14 GW in the second half of 2026, 26 GW in 2027, and 14 GW in 2028. The growth is concentrated in very large solar-plus-storage hybrid projects: the Bellefield Solar and Energy Storage Farm (500 MW of PV paired with 500 MW of storage, operational since December 2025 in CAISO, with a reported plan to double both by November 2026 that would make it the largest U.S. storage facility); the Manatee Solar Energy Center in Florida (409 MW of storage, operational since 2021, the second-largest project); and Gemini Solar Hybrid in Nevada (380 MW of storage, operational 2024). For a power-electronics supplier, these figures indicate a fast-expanding installed base and a substantial reported pipeline of new capacity — a leading indicator of demand for conversion and related equipment at multi-hundred-megawatt scale.

**What the figures cannot establish (S1).** First, addressable supplier revenue: the source reports nameplate power capacity only, not equipment prices, project costs, or any supplier's share of a project, and megawatts are not dollars. Second, project returns: the article offers a qualitative mechanism — operators store power when wholesale prices are low and discharge when prices are high, capturing larger profits — but no costs, contracts, or margins. Third, certainty: the 54 GW of additions are what operators "anticipate" and "plan," and the source itself conditions the Bellefield expansion on "if those plans materialize." Fourth, storage duration and system configuration, which materially shape both energy capacity and power-electronics content per project, are not reported.

**Opportunity vs. caution.** The strongest opportunity signal is the scale and continuity of reported additions: 8.3 GW installed in six months of 2026, plus 54 GW of planned capacity led by 26 GW in 2027, on top of three years of 70% average annual growth. The strongest reason for caution is that this is planned, not committed, capacity, and the same source provides no pricing, duration, or margin data — so neither revenue nor returns can be quantified from S1 alone.

**Recommendation.** Authorize a limited market-discovery effort rather than a larger commercial commitment. A bounded exercise should (i) track which reported planned projects move into construction or final investment decision and toward contracted equipment procurement; (ii) map where power-electronics equipment is specified and procured, using the large named hybrid projects as reference cases; and (iii) source the missing commercial variables — prices, durations, and revenue structures — beyond this memo's scope. The source justifies acting on the signal; it does not justify sizing a commitment.

**Next evidence that would change the recommendation.** The most decisive next data point would be evidence that a material share of the 26 GW planned for 2027 has converted from "planned" to under construction or final investment decision with equipment procurement underway — for example, updated EIA inventory data reclassifying planned capacity, or disclosed procurement awards to power-equipment vendors. A near-term credibility test also exists: whether the Bellefield expansion actually becomes operational by November 2026 as reported. Sustained conversion of plans into built capacity would support scaling up; persistent gaps would confirm the caution case and keep engagement at the discovery stage.
