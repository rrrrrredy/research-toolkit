**MEMORANDUM**

TO: Strategy Lead
FROM: Research Desk
DATE: August 10, 2026
RE: U.S. Utility-Scale Battery-Storage Market — Implications of Latest EIA Capacity Data

**Scope and basis**

This memo relies solely on the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) article published August 7, 2026 (S1), which draws on the Preliminary Monthly Electric Generator Inventory. It is a bounded, single-source desk assessment prepared for internal decision-making; it is not investment advice and does not incorporate live market data, supplier pricing, or competitive intelligence. No independent review was conducted.

**Bottom line**

The EIA data confirm a large, rapidly growing U.S. utility-scale battery-storage fleet with a substantial reported pipeline of planned additions. This supports authorizing a limited market-discovery effort to evaluate whether our power-electronics products can compete. It does not support a larger commercial commitment, because the source cannot establish addressable supplier revenue, equipment content per megawatt, or project returns.

**What the figures show**

U.S. utility-scale battery-storage capacity grew at an annual average rate of 70% over the last three years (S1). Operational capacity reached 43.6 GW by the end of 2025. Operators added another 8.3 GW in the first six months of 2026, bringing total nameplate capacity to nearly 52 GW (S1).

Looking ahead, operators report plans to bring an additional 54 GW online over the next two and a half years: 14 GW in the second half of 2026, 26 GW in 2027, and 14 GW in 2028 (S1). If these plans materialize, installed capacity would roughly double from mid-2026 levels by the end of 2028.

**What this implies for demand**

For a power-electronics supplier, the figures signal sustained and growing demand for the conversion, control, and integration equipment that battery-storage projects require. The 8.3 GW added in a single half-year, combined with a 54 GW reported pipeline, indicates that procurement activity is likely to continue at scale through at least 2028. The concentration of large projects — such as the Bellefield Solar and Energy Storage Farm in California (500 MW storage, with plans to double), the Manatee Solar Energy Center in Florida (409 MW), and the Gemini Solar Hybrid in Nevada (380 MW) — suggests that utility-scale and solar-plus-storage developments are the primary demand drivers and that individual projects can represent meaningful equipment orders (S1). The EIA also notes that solar-plus-storage facilities enable arbitrage — storing power when wholesale prices are low and discharging when prices are high — which underpins the economic rationale for these projects (S1).

**What the figures cannot establish**

The EIA data are capacity figures, not market-value figures. They cannot establish:

- Addressable supplier revenue. The source contains no pricing, revenue, or market-size data for power electronics or any other equipment category.
- Equipment content per megawatt. The source reports nameplate power capacity (MW) but not storage duration (hours) or energy capacity (GWh). A megawatt of storage with different durations implies different power-electronics and battery content, so equipment demand cannot be derived from MW alone.
- Project returns or margins. No cost, revenue, or profitability data are provided.
- Probability of pipeline realization. The 54 GW of planned additions reflects operator-reported intentions to EIA, not firm commitments, construction starts, or financial closes. Plans can be delayed, reduced, or cancelled.
- Competitive dynamics. The source does not identify existing suppliers, market share, or barriers to entry.

**Strongest opportunity signal vs. strongest reason for caution**

The strongest opportunity signal is the combination of demonstrated momentum and a large reported pipeline: 70% average annual growth over three years, 8.3 GW added in H1 2026 alone, and 54 GW of planned additions that would roughly double the fleet (S1). This is consistent with a market in which a new supplier could find growing demand over a multi-year horizon.

The strongest reason for caution is that the forward-looking 54 GW is entirely plan-based. EIA explicitly attributes these figures to "plans they report to us" (S1). Without evidence that these projects have reached financial close or begun construction, the pipeline may overstate near-term equipment demand. The historical growth rate, while impressive, is backward-looking and does not guarantee continuation.

**Recommendation**

Authorize a limited market-discovery effort. The confirmed growth and reported pipeline justify a bounded investigation into where our power-electronics capabilities align with project requirements, which project developers and engineering-procurement-construction firms are active, and what equipment specifications and certifications are required. Do not authorize a larger commercial commitment at this stage; the source does not provide the revenue, margin, or competitive-positioning evidence needed to justify one.

**Next evidence that would change the recommendation**

The recommendation would shift toward a larger commitment if we obtained: (1) storage-duration data (GWh) to convert power capacity into energy capacity and estimate equipment content; (2) power-electronics pricing and supplier market-share data to size addressable revenue; (3) evidence that a material share of the 54 GW pipeline has reached financial close or construction start; and (4) project-level cost or return benchmarks. Conversely, evidence of widespread project cancellations or delays would reinforce the limited-effort recommendation.

**Source**

S1: U.S. Energy Information Administration, "Battery storage capacity averaged 70% growth over the last three years," August 7, 2026. Data source: Preliminary Monthly Electric Generator Inventory.
