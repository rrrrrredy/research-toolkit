P1L1: Minimize Compressed Air Leaks
P1L2: Leaks are a significant source of wasted energy in a compressed air system, often wasting
P1L3: as much as 20-30% of the compressor’s output. Compressed air leaks can also contribute
P1L4: to problems with system operations, including:
P1L5: • Fluctuating system pressure, which can cause air tools and other air-operated 
P1L6: equipment to function less efficiently, possibly affecting production
P1L7: • Excess compressor capacity, resulting in higher than necessary costs
P1L8: • Decreased service life and increased maintenance of supply equipment (including the
P1L9: compressor package) due to unnecessary cycling and increased run time.
P1L10: Although leaks can occur in any part of the system, the most common problem areas are:
P1L11: couplings, hoses, tubes, fittings, pipe joints, quick disconnects, FRLs (filter, regulator, and
P1L12: lubricator), condensate traps, valves, flanges, packings, thread sealants, and point of use
P1L13: devices. Leakage rates are a function of the supply pressure in an uncontrolled system
P1L14: and increase with higher system pressures. Leakage rates are also proportional to the
P1L15: square of the orifice diameter. (See table below.)
P1L16: Leak Detection
P1L17: The best way to detect leaks is to use an ultrasonic acoustic detector, which can 
P1L18: recognize high frequency hissing sounds associated with air leaks. These portable units
P1L19: are very easy to use. Costs and sensitivities vary, so test before you buy. A simpler
P1L20: method is to apply soapy water with a paintbrush to suspect areas. Although reliable,
P1L21: this method can be time consuming and messy.

P1L23: Suggested Actions
P1L24: • Fixing leaks once is not enough.
P1L25: Incorporate a leak prevention
P1L26: program into your facility’s
P1L27: operations. It should include
P1L28: identification and tagging,
P1L29: tracking, repair, verification,
P1L30: and employee involvement. 
P1L31: Set a reasonable target for cost-
P1L32: effective leak reduction—5-10%
P1L33: of total system flow is typical
P1L34: for industrial facilities.
P1L35: • Once leaks are repaired, 
P1L36: re-evaluate your compressed air
P1L37: system supply. Work with a
P1L38: compressed air systems specialist
P1L39: to adjust compressor controls.
P1L40: Also look at alternatives to
P1L41: some compressed air uses. If a
P1L42: compressor can be turned off, 
P1L43: benefits include cost savings
P1L44: and a system backup.

P2L74: Example
P2L75: A chemical plant undertook a leak prevention program following a compressed air
P2L76: audit at their facility. Leaks, approximately equivalent to different orifice sizes, were
P2L77: found as follows: 100 leaks of 1/32" at 90 psig, 50 leaks of 1/16" at 90 psig, and 
P2L78: 10 leaks of 1/4" at 100 psig. Calculate the annual cost savings if these leaks were
P2L79: eliminated. Assume 7000 annual operating hours, an aggregate electric rate of 
P2L80: $0.05 /kWh, and compressed air generation requirement of approximately 
P2L81: 18 kW /100 cfm.
P2L82: Cost savings = # of leaks x leakage rate (cfm) x kW/cfm x # of hours x $/kWh
P2L83: Using values of the leakage rates from the above table and assuming sharp-edged
P2L84: orifices:
P2L85: Cost savings from 1/32" leaks = 100 x 1.5 x 0.61 x 0.18 x 7000 x 0.05 = $5,765
P2L86: Cost savings from 1/16" leaks = 50 x 5.9 x 0.61 x 0.18 x 7000 x 0.05 = $11,337
P2L87: Cost savings from 1/4" leaks = 10 x 104 x 0.61 x 0.18 x 7000 x 0.05 = $39,967
P2L88: Total cost savings from eliminating these leaks = $57,069
P2L89: Note that the savings from the elimination of just 10 leaks of 1/4" account for
P2L90: almost 70% of the overall savings. As leaks are identified, it is important to 
P2L91: prioritize them and fix the largest ones first.
